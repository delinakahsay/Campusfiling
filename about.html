About the project

Why a tax site, and how we sized the problem

Campus Filing is an information systems capstone at the George Washington University School of Business.

The problem

Students occupy a narrow band of tax situations that consumer software handles badly. They are usually dependents, which changes the standard deduction in a way few products explain. They receive a 1098-T that belongs on someone else's return. They frequently have scholarship money that is taxable and gig income with nothing withheld. Many attend school in a state they are not a resident of.

None of that is complicated once explained. Almost none of it is explained anywhere a student will find it.

The consequence

Two failure modes. Students who are owed money do not file and lose it permanently after three years. Students who do file get routed into paid tiers for forms that are free elsewhere, or make errors that cost them a credit or trigger a rejected return.

How the product answers it

A guided walkthrough that produces a specific, printable plan rather than general advice, paired with reference guides for the situations that recur. The tool deliberately stops short of preparing a return, because the free filing infrastructure already exists and the gap is knowing how to use it.

Methodology and data sources

The analysis component sizes the affected population and the dollar value at stake rather than asserting either. Sources used:

  • IRS Statistics of Income, individual returns and ZIP code data, for filing rates and refund distributions by income band
  • IRS Free File program statistics and the IRS Direct File closure notice of November 2025, for free-filing uptake against the eligible population and for what the shutdown removed
  • IPEDS enrollment data, for the undergraduate and international student population by institution
  • National Society of Accountants fee survey, for the cost of the paid alternative
  • Federal Trade Commission filings in the matter of Intuit, for documentation of free-tier practices

What the data showed

The finding that shaped the product came from the IRS ZIP code file for tax year 2022, read at the lowest income band, the one nearly every filing student sits in. We expected to find students paying preparers for work they could do free. The opposite is true.

  • In ZIP codes built around large universities, 68% of filers earning under $25,000 used no paid preparer and no volunteer clinic. Nationally in the same income band that figure is 48%.
  • Those students pay a preparer at 28.6%, well under the 49.3% national rate for the band. They are not choosing badly between paid options, they are working alone.
  • Free volunteer clinics reached 3.5% of them. Nationally, VITA and TCE prepared 2.3 million returns against 42.9 million filed in the band.
  • Education credits appear on 8.0% of low-income returns in campus ZIP codes, against 2.9% nationally.
  • The average refund in the band is $927 nationally and $830 in campus ZIP codes, which is the sum at stake per student.

That is the gap this product fills. Not cheaper preparation, but guidance for people who are already filing by themselves.

Method: national figures are built from the state-total rows of the SOI file rather than by summing ZIP codes, because small ZIP-level cells are suppressed and summing them undercounts. The campus figure pools 18 ZIP codes containing large universities, each with at least 1,000 returns in the band. Column meanings were checked against published national totals before use: the paid-preparer share resolves to 53.1% against the IRS's own reported figure of about 53%, and the earned income credit to 23.5 million returns. A campus ZIP code captures everyone who files from that address, not students alone, which is why the comparison is drawn against the same income band nationally rather than against the general population.

Scope decisions worth defending

Two choices were made deliberately and will look like gaps if not explained.

The walkthrough ends in a step-by-step worksheet keyed to the filing product the student chose, naming the screen to open and the figure to enter at each step. It deliberately names screens and schedules rather than numbered form lines, because the current year's Form 1040 is not published until December and state forms renumber constantly. The one exception is a reference that has been stable since the 2022 redesign, Schedule 1 line 8r for taxable scholarship money, which is named because students otherwise never find it.

The build asks nothing about who a student is beyond the email address an optional account needs. There is no field for a Social Security number, a bank account, an employer, an address or a school, so there is nothing of that kind to store. Documents are read on the device and are never written anywhere, with or without an account.

An account is optional and stores one thing: the walkthrough answers a student chooses to save, one plan per tax year, so a prior year can be reopened rather than retyped. It exists because the institutional licence model requires it, since a university paying per enrolled student needs each student to have a login before there is anything to license. Access is enforced by Row Level Security in the database rather than by this site's code, so a mistake in the site cannot show one student another student's plan. For the capstone demonstration we use practice figures rather than real student income.

Course

Information systems capstone, GW School of Business. The build demonstrates systems analysis, database design, front end development, and data analysis in one deliverable.

Stack

Static HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript with no build step or framework dependency. Every tax figure resolves from a single constants module so annual updates touch one file.

Optional accounts run on Supabase Postgres, reached only from this site's own serverless functions, with Row Level Security enforcing per-student access in the database itself.

Accuracy

Figures reflect tax year 2026 as published in IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32. Dependency rules follow IRC section 152 and Publication 501. Education provisions follow Publication 970.
