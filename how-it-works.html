How it works

Three steps, about ten minutes

The walkthrough is nine short sections. You can leave and come back, and nothing is saved anywhere, so there is no wrong answer to worry about committing to.

Step one

Answer questions about your year

Status, age, whether your parents supported you, what money came in, what school cost, which states you lived and worked in. Plain questions, no jargon, with the reasoning shown next to each one.

Step two

Upload the forms you have

Digital PDFs are read in your browser and the amounts proposed back for you to check against the paper before anything is applied. Photos and scans are listed but typed in by hand. Files are not transmitted anywhere.

Step three

Get a plan and go file

A printable list of your forms, your credits, your state filing order, and a recommendation for where to file at no cost. Then you go do it.

What the nine sections cover

Everything below changes your answer, which is why each one is asked.

SectionWhat it determines
Your statusWhich return you file. Almost everyone files Form 1040, but F-1 and J-1 students in their first five years file Form 1040-NR instead, and common software cannot produce it. Being here legally does not automatically make you a resident for tax purposes, which is why this is asked first.
About youAge, marital status, and enrollment, which drive the dependency tests and every education credit.
DependencyWhether your parents can claim you, and the reduced standard deduction that comes with it.
DocumentsReads the amounts out of the PDFs you upload, proposes them for checking, and tracks which forms you still need to find.
IncomeWages, tips, gig profit, fellowship and research stipends, interest, dividends, stock and crypto sales, unemployment.
School moneyTwo things only, and most of what you paid is neither. Scholarship and stipend money above tuition, required fees, books and supplies is taxable income. Student loan interest is deductible if nobody claims you. Rent, meal plans, travel, and tuition you paid yourself do not produce a deduction, though tuition does support an education credit for whoever claims you.
Other situationsMarketplace health insurance, retirement contributions, foreign accounts, mid-year moves, unfiled prior years.
States and softwareWhich state returns you owe, in what order, and which filing product fits your forms without charging you.
Your planThe output: your dependency answer, what to gather, your state filing order, and a step-by-step worksheet keyed to the software you picked. Printable.

What this does not do

Being clear about the edges is more useful than overpromising.

  • It does not prepare or transmit a return. You file with the IRS or a filing product, and this tells you how to get there without paying.
  • It does not give line-by-line entries like "put this on line 11a." Line numbers move between tax years and state forms renumber constantly, so the plan names the form and the schedule in plain language instead. Where a line reference is stable and load bearing, such as Schedule 1 line 8r for taxable scholarship money, the plan names it.
  • It does not handle business entities, rental property, or trusts. If your situation involves any of those, a volunteer tax clinic is a better fit.
  • It does not replace advice from a licensed preparer for anything genuinely unusual.

If you would rather talk to a person

The IRS Volunteer Income Tax Assistance program places certified volunteers at free clinics nationwide, and many universities host one staffed by accounting students. Bring the checklist this tool produces and the appointment goes much faster.
