Glossary

Alphabetical. One line each, written the way you would explain it out loud.

Adjusted gross income (AGI)
Your total income minus a short list of adjustments such as student loan interest. Software asks for last year's AGI to verify your identity when you e-file.
American Opportunity Credit
An education credit worth up to $2,500 per student for the first four years of undergraduate study. Claimed by whoever claims the student as a dependent.
Dependent
Someone who can be claimed on another person's return. For students this is almost always the qualifying child test. Being claimed reduces your standard deduction and moves education credits to your parents.
Domicile
Your permanent legal home. Attending school in another state does not change it. Your license, voter registration, and permanent address determine it.
Earned income
Money you worked for. Wages, tips, and net self-employment profit. Taxable scholarship money counts as earned income for the standard deduction calculation.
Form 1040
The standard individual federal tax return. Page one contains the checkbox acknowledging that someone can claim you as a dependent.
Form 1040-NR
The return filed by nonresident aliens, including F-1 and J-1 students in their first five years. Most consumer software cannot produce it.
Form 1040-X
The amended return, used to correct a return you already filed. There is no penalty for correcting something yourself.
Form 8843
A statement of exempt individual status that F-1 and J-1 students must file annually, even with no income at all.
Form 8863
The form used to claim education credits. Attached to whichever return claims the student.
Lifetime Learning Credit
An education credit worth up to $2,000 per return. No four year limit and no half-time enrollment requirement, which makes it the fallback when the American Opportunity Credit runs out.
Nonresident return
A state return filed where you earned income but did not live. Filed before your resident return so the credit for tax paid to another state calculates correctly.
Qualified tuition and fees
Tuition and fees required for enrollment. Room, board, transportation, and optional equipment do not qualify, which is what makes some scholarship money taxable.
Qualifying child
The dependency test that covers most students. Under 19, or under 24 and a full-time student, living with the parent more than half the year, and not providing more than half their own support.
Refundable credit
A credit that can produce a refund even when you owe no tax. Most credits are nonrefundable and only reduce tax you already owe.
Resident return
The state return filed where you are domiciled. Reports all of your income regardless of where you earned it.
Schedule C
Where self-employment income and expenses are reported. Required once gig work produces a profit.
Schedule SE
Where self-employment tax is calculated, at 15.3 percent of net profit. Applies once net profit reaches $400.
Self-employment tax
Social Security and Medicare tax paid by people who work for themselves. An employee splits this with their employer. A gig worker pays both halves.
Standard deduction
The flat amount subtracted from your income before tax is calculated. $16,100 for a single filer in 2026, reduced for dependents to earned income plus $450 with a $1,350 floor.
Substantial presence test
The day-counting test that determines whether a visa holder is a resident for tax purposes. F-1 and J-1 students are exempt from counting for five calendar years.
Support
Everything spent on a person's living costs: housing, food, tuition, medical care, transportation, clothing. Scholarships are excluded entirely. Loans in your own name count as your support.
Unearned income
Money you did not work for. Interest, dividends, capital gains, and unemployment. A dependent must file once unearned income passes $1,350.
VITA
Volunteer Income Tax Assistance, an IRS program placing certified volunteers at free tax clinics. Many universities host one staffed by accounting students.
Withholding
Tax taken out of your paycheck and sent to the IRS on your behalf. Reported in box 2 of your W-2. If more was withheld than you owe, the difference is your refund.
